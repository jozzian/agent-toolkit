# Analytical Essayist Tone

Apply this tone to the piece of writing at hand: an essay, an explainer,
a LinkedIn or Medium-style article, or an analytical think-piece that
examines a concept, compares systems (countries, regulations,
technologies), or questions the use of a popular term. Follow every rule
below on the content you are asked to write. This tone is not for casual
chat, marketing copy, or short social posts.

A formal, structured, mildly skeptical voice for essays that take a term,
policy, or comparison and work out what it actually means. Derived from
five full pieces by the essayist whose voice this tone captures
(the innovation-job-titles intro post, the
vegetational-concepts-in-innovation essay, the Swiss Kantönligeist piece,
the South Korea "Galapagos of Asia" piece, and the Hong Kong / Greater
Bay Area piece) plus the titles and summaries of the remaining work on
Korea, Switzerland, ESG/CSRD regulation, and AI. LinkedIn's robots policy
blocked automated access to the ESG and AI pieces, so those informed the
topic range but not the sentence-level rules below.

## Voice in one paragraph

The writer treats a familiar word or claim as something that deserves
interrogation rather than repetition. An essay usually opens by naming
how widespread a term or practice has become, asks what it actually rests
on, traces its origin or first use, and then works forward through the
tension or cost that the popular version glosses over. The tone stays
measured and even where the underlying claim is critical: it argues by
laying out evidence and structure, not by asserting outrage. There is no
first-person confession and almost no "I" outside of pieces explicitly
framed as a personal declaration.

## What to do

1. No contractions. Write "does not," "it is," "cannot," "there is."
   Never "doesn't," "it's," "can't," "there's." This applies everywhere,
   including headers and quoted paraphrase.
2. No em dashes. Use a hyphen with spaces, a colon, or a semicolon
   instead of an em dash for asides and appositions.
3. Third person, low personal presence. Default to an impersonal,
   analytical narrator. Reserve "I" for pieces explicitly framed as a
   personal take (an opinion piece, a declaration), and even then keep
   it to a sentence or two, not a running first-person narrative.
   The brief credibility anecdote permitted by rule 8 is a separate,
   bounded exception, not a license for a first-person narrator.
4. One precise number early. Open factual pieces with a concrete figure
   (a percentage, a count, a date) rather than a general claim. "With
   around 40,000 newly founded entities a year, Switzerland ranks..."
   not "Switzerland is a very innovative country."
5. Name the source of a claim. Attribute definitions and data to a
   person, index, or report by name ("Arthur Tansley, a botanist... was
   the first to put the word to paper in 1935"; "the Global
   Entrepreneurship Index"). Footnote-style citations ([1], [2]) are
   acceptable for a piece with several quoted sources.
6. Define borrowed terms before using them. If the piece leans on a
   concept from outside its home field (ecosystem from botany, creative
   destruction from Schumpeter, Kantönligeist from Swiss politics), give
   a short, sourced definition before putting the term to work.
7. Complicate before concluding. Do not just describe a trend. Test it.
   A typical move: state the popular view, show what a closer look
   reveals, then explain why the popular view still falls short (or,
   occasionally, why it deserves more credit than it gets).
8. A first-person anecdote may open a section, but it is used as
   evidence, not as feeling. "Arriving in Seoul a month ago, I have been
   welcomed by a vibrant ecosystem..." and "Attending the Hong Kong
   Fintech week as Head of Business Development of AAAccell... provided
   me with great insights into a rapidly transforming ecosystem" are both
   used to establish the writer's standing to make the claim that
   follows, then the piece moves straight back to impersonal analysis. Do
   not linger on the anecdote or draw an emotional conclusion from it.
9. Hyperlink density. Nearly every statistic, named report, or named
   organization in the source material carries a link. When writing for a
   medium that supports links, attach one to each factual claim that has
   a real source; when it does not, name the source in the sentence
   instead of dropping the claim's specificity.

## Structure

- Bolded lead paragraph. The opening paragraph is set in bold and
  functions as an abstract: it states the core fact or claim of the whole
  piece in two to four sentences, before any section heading appears.
  Write this paragraph last, once the argument is settled, so it can
  compress the whole essay.
- Optional mini table of contents. For pieces with three or more
  sections, list the section titles as a short numbered list directly
  under the title, before the bolded lead paragraph. Shorter pieces
  (under roughly 600 words) skip this.
- ## headers as section titles, not numbered headers, each naming a
  specific idea rather than a generic label ("A unique Ecosystem," "The
  shadow of Kantönligeist," "Connecting different worlds," not
  "Background" or "Analysis").
- Aim for three to six sections following this shape:
  1. Framing / hook: already covered by the bolded lead paragraph; the
     first section proper can start closer to the ground, often with a
     first-person anecdote or a specific event (a conference attended, a
     first month in a new city, a report just released).
  2. Origin or definition: trace where the term, figure, or policy comes
     from, with a named source and, where useful, a quoted definition.
  3. Complication: introduce the friction: what the popular usage
     misses, what data or comparison undercuts it, what cost or
     contradiction it creates.
  4. Illustration: a concrete example, comparison, or case that makes
     the complication legible (comparing two countries, walking through a
     numbered breakdown of the actors or steps involved).
  5. Closing section: restates the core claim in a sentence or two and
     states plainly where the writer lands. Longer, source-heavy pieces
     label this "Summary"; shorter pieces fold it into the last section's
     final lines without a separate label, often ending on a
     forward-looking or evaluative image ("place Hong Kong at the heart of
     a new dawn of finance in Asia") rather than a flat recap.

## Sentence-level habits

- Long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences are normal, but each one
  should still resolve to a single clear claim. Favor "Although X, Y" and
  "As a result, Z" over strings of short declarative sentences.
- Use formal connectors to move between ideas: however, therefore, as a
  result, consequently, in addition, nonetheless.
- Numbered in-line lists are used to break down parallel factors: "(1)
  the link between individuals... (2) the relationships connecting... (3)
  the intersection between..."
- Hyphenated compound modifiers are frequent and welcome: resource-scarce,
  innovation-promotion, vegetational-concept, systems-level.
- Rhetorical questions belong in the opening section only, not scattered
  through the body.
- Light irony is permitted in subheadings and title-level wordplay (e.g.,
  pairing a technical term with an everyday image: "the shadow of the
  Kantönligeist," "the Galapagos of Asia") but never in the analytical
  body text itself.

## Vocabulary register

Latinate and systems-level vocabulary over casual phrasing. These
substitutions apply where the technical term adds precision; an ordinary
noun that already carries the meaning stays correct and should not be
inflated:

| Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|
| exceedingly, disproportionately, systemic | super, really, a lot |
| entities, actors, incumbents | people, companies (used generically) |
| coordination, harmonization | teamwork |
| justify, warrant | make sense |
| abundance, scarcity | lots, not enough |

Avoid hype adjectives (game-changing, groundbreaking, revolutionary)
unless directly quoting a source that uses them, in which case attribute
the word to that source rather than adopting it as narration.

One coined or borrowed image per piece is welcome and gives the essay a
memorable handle: "zombie startup" for a firm kept alive by grants rather
than revenue, "jungle of readily available government grants" for an
overcrowded support landscape, "melting pot of civilisations" for a
cross-border economic zone. Introduce the image once, explain it in a
sentence, then reuse the same phrase rather than varying it, and do not pile
up multiple new metaphors in the same piece.

## Do and avoid examples

Do:

> With over one hundred public innovation-promotion agencies operating
> across cantonal, regional, and national levels, Switzerland's
> institutional infrastructure is both a strength and, on closer
> inspection, a source of waste.

(In a published piece the count itself carries a named source or link,
per rule 5; the example compresses that away to show the sentence shape.)

Avoid (contraction, vague, no source):

> Switzerland's got a ton of innovation agencies, and honestly it's kind
> of a mess.

Do:

> Arthur Tansley, a botanist researching systems of organic communities,
> was the first to put the term to paper in 1935.

Avoid (unsourced, casual):

> The word "ecosystem" has been around forever and everyone just uses it
> now.

## Checklist before finalizing a piece in this tone

- [ ] Zero contractions anywhere in the text, including headers
- [ ] Zero em dashes; hyphens, colons, or semicolons used instead
- [ ] Opening paragraph contains a concrete number, date, or named source
- [ ] Every borrowed or technical term is defined once before reuse
- [ ] At least one section complicates or tests the popular claim rather
      than only describing it
- [ ] Closing section states a clear verdict in one or two sentences
- [ ] First-person "I" appears only as a personal take or as a brief
      credibility anecdote at the start of a section, never as the
      piece's ongoing narrator
- [ ] The opening paragraph is bolded and compresses the piece's core
      claim
- [ ] Section headers name a specific idea, not a generic label
- [ ] At most one coined or borrowed image is introduced, and it is
      reused rather than replaced by a second metaphor

## Invocation

This tone applies when the user asks to write "in my style," "in the
essayist's voice," or requests an essay, explainer, LinkedIn/Medium-style
article, or analytical, comparative, or think-piece, and no other tone is
specified. An agent configuration may make it the default for those
genres; whether it runs by default is a property of each tool's or chat's
own configuration, not of this file. Pasting this file into a chat that
has no access to this repository may establish the tone for that
conversation, subject to how that interface actually handles pasted
instructions; some interfaces hold pasted instructions for the rest of
the conversation, others do not, and this file by itself cannot guarantee
either behavior.
